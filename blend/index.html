1.1

What does mind go before?

The Pāli dhammā (plural) is the word your ruling says to translate by context. Here the context is what we live through: the states of mind out of which we speak and act, which the wheel and the ox then make tangible. “All things” and “all phenomena” read more widely and can sound as if mind makes the outer world, which the verse does not need to claim.

You said Leaning to “experience”: contextual, and it makes sense of what the verse is saying. Also offered all phenomena, the objects of mind, all things.

Or write your own version of the line.

1.2

Mind-ruled, mind-made, or mind-swift?

Pāli manoseṭṭhā manomayā: mind-foremost, mind-made. Patna, Gāndhārī, Sanskrit and Tibetan have manojavā where the Pāli has manomayā. Manojava is a common compound for ‘having the speed of mind’, swift as thought: these states arise as fast as the mind that leads them. Our earlier Patna, Gāndhārī and Sanskrit columns rendered it ‘mind drives them’, which is why you did not find ‘swift’ there. Seṭṭha means foremost or best, so ‘ruled’ and ‘supreme’ are both fair. The Pāli's three hits of mind (mano-, mano-, mano-) are the original's poetry, and Literal+ allows keeping it now rather than in the later style pass.

You said Prefers the Pāli for now because “swift as mind” is unclear. Likes “ruled”; finds “chief” strange in English; wants the Pāli's terse three-fold mind, carrying the weight of age-old authority.

Or write your own version of the line.

The two readers’ line-by-line notes

  1. T4 Akkocchi maṁ avadhi maṁ, · readers said T4 and T3

    'He abused me, he struck me,

    • Sanskrit, Tibetan, Chinese variant avadhi 'struck' (Pāli, Patna) vs verbal abuse only: Sanskrit avocan 'spoke', Tibetan ཁ་ཟེར/གཤེ 'scold/revile', Chinese (T212) 罵 'revile'.
    • Chinese structure Chinese is T212 (Chuyao jing), an Udānavarga-family text, so it is not independent of sa/bo; it is phrased as a condition ('if someone').
    • Sanskrit, Tibetan variant Sanskrit avocan 'they spoke' replaces avadhi 'he struck'; Tibetan has two verbs of speech (ཁ་ཟེར་བ, གཤེ), so the Udānavarga line has verbal abuse only, no striking.
    • Chinese translation T212 人若罵我 'if someone reviles me' renders only the first verb, as a condition.

  2. T3 ajini maṁ ahāsi me;

    he defeated me, he robbed me' —

    • Sanskrit wording ajāpayet (causative of ji, 'cause to lose, deprive') ≈ ahāsi 'robbed'; my parse of ajāpayet is from Pāli jāpeti and is not certain.
    • Tibetan, Chinese translation Tibetan and Chinese render both verbs as defeat (བདག་ཕམ…ཕམ་བྱེད; 勝我不勝), losing 'robbed'; plausibly a rendering of the causative of ji.
    • Sanskrit, Tibetan wording ahāsi 'robbed' vs ajāpayet 'caused [me] to lose' (causative of ji/jyā); Tibetan ཕམ་བྱེད 'caused defeat/loss'. Close in sense (loss of goods vs being worsted).
    • Chinese translation 勝我不勝 'defeated me, I did not win' (T212 commentary glosses it as winning or not by the Dharma); robbery is absent.
    • Tibetan structure Tibetan ends the quoted complaint with ཞེས 'thus', marking lines 1-2 as a quotation.

  3. T3 Ye ca taṁ upanayhanti, · readers said T3 and T1

    those who bind it up (harbour that [thought]),

    • Sanskrit wording atra 'here/in this case' added; no object pronoun.
    • Chinese translation 快意從者 is hard to parse; it may mean 'those who indulge [these feelings]' (≈ harbour) or something else. Low confidence.

  4. T2 veraṁ tesaṁ na sammati. · readers said T1 and T2

    their enmity is not appeased.

    • Chinese translation 怨終不息 'resentment in the end does not cease'.

5

How hostility is stilled

Your ruling keeps “hostility”. Keeping it singular avoids “hostilities”. What is open is the cure: Pāli and Patna say non-hostility; the Chinese, by not resenting; the Sanskrit and Tibetan, by patience (kṣānti). B is the Udānavarga's reading, a real Blend choice: it names the virtue that the negation points to.

You said Hostility's plural and negation are unclear in English; the base, plural and negation need to form an intelligible set.

7.1

What the undisciplined man dwells on

Subhānupassī is one who keeps seeing things, above all the body, as lovely and desirable: the habit that feeds lust. The Chinese says so outright (行見身淨, ‘seeing the body as pure’). Its opposite in verse 8, asubha, is the classic meditation on the body's unlovely parts. So the verse is about fixing on sensual allure, not about loving the beautiful in Plato's sense.

You said Has always found this line strange: why would focusing on beauty be a problem? Maybe it means attractive as in pleasant.

Or write your own version of the line.

7.5

Who or what overpowers him

Pāli and Patna: Māra. Gāndhārī and Sanskrit: passion (rāga); the Tibetan: desires, in the plural. Chinese: ‘the evil’. Like the wind, it is a force that topples; Māra adds the tempter who makes vain things look worth having. “Temptation” carries both readings. The book-wide default for Māra is a separate question (Words: Māra).

You said The meaning is succumbing to vain temptations. “Passion” fails because we honour some passions (for one's dreams, one's lover, the Passion of Christ); “Māra” alone carries the tempter but not the force.

Or write your own version of the line.

Words for the whole book

Choices that apply wherever the word appears. Settled ones are kept with your reasons.

dhamma

dhamma (verses 1–2, 20)

settled

One Indic word with many senses: what bears or upholds, the nature of things, states of mind, the teaching.

Translate by context, not with one fixed word. In verses 1–2 the context is lived experience: the verse sets up the wheel following the ox. (Owner, 2026-11-09)

vera

vera: “hostility”

settled

Enmity held against someone (verses 3–5). Options were enmity, hostility, hatred.

“Hostility” (B). “Hatred” is kept for dosa; “enmity” is less well understood; the Chinese “resentment” is interesting but sits with the one receiving hostility. Condition: base, plural and negation must read as an intelligible set in English (see verse 5). (Owner, 2026-11-09)

stain

The stained robe (verses 9–10)

settled

The Pāli puns on kasāva, stain, and kāsāva, the dyed ochre robe.

Keep the pun (A). “Impurities” sat oddly after verse 7–8's warning about attractiveness; stain/robe avoids that. (Owner, 2026-11-09)

sanantano

sanantano: “age-old”, not “eternal” (verse 5)

Sanantano means from of old, perpetual. Neither the Chinese nor the Tibetan says eternal.

teaching

dhamma as the teaching (verse 20)

Where dhamma clearly means the Buddha's teaching: plain English, or the word itself.

mara

Māra, across the book

Māra recurs through the Dhammapada, sometimes as a figure (his realm, his army, his flower-tipped arrows), sometimes as a force, as in verses 7–8. This sets the default; a verse can still depart (see 7.5).

samana

samaṇa: “renunciant”, not “recluse” (verses 19–20)

A samaṇa is someone who has left household life to seek liberation by spiritual striving; the Buddha's monks were samaṇas, as were other wanderers. “Recluse” suggests a hermit; “mendicant” is already our word for bhikkhu, the begging monk; “renunciant” names what the samaṇa has done.

Principles

How the first draft weighs the traditions. They set the starting point and flag departures; they never stop you choosing the best reading. Say “agree”, or change any of them.

  • rule-1 Count traditions by family. The Tibetan translates the Sanskrit Udānavarga, so the two count as one voice. Where the Tibetan alone departs from its Sanskrit (verse 1's “head cut off by a wheel”), that is a translation matter.
  • rule-2 A lone reading starts as the outsider. A reading found in one tradition only does not go into the first draft. It can still be chosen on its merits, as the best reading; the choice is then marked as such.
  • rule-3 The Chinese is a reading too. Where the Chinese says the same thing in its own idiom, it counts as agreement. Where it has its own image, the image is a candidate for the best reading (verse 2's “shadow following the body”), never thrown out by default.
  • rule-4 Keep every Pāli line. Partial parallels inform but never add or remove lines. Where other traditions lack a line, that is noted, not treated as evidence against it.
  • rule-5 Family against family: decide verse by verse. When Pāli and Patna stand against Gāndhārī, Sanskrit and Tibetan, the evidence is roughly even, so it is a question for you in that verse.
  • rule-6 Same idea, different word. The first draft uses the word with the widest support, the Pāli's on a tie. You may prefer another tradition's image when it is better.
Differences noted so far (10)

  • N-001 1.2, 2.2: The Pāli alone says the mind's states are “made of mind” (manomayā). Patna, Gāndhārī, Sanskrit and Tibetan all say “swift as mind” (manojavā).

    The most quoted line of the Dhammapada is, on this evidence, the outlier. “Made of mind” invites the reading that mind constructs experience; “swift as mind” stresses the speed with which mind leads. The teaching that mind comes first stands in every tradition either way.

  • N-002 5.3: Enmity is ended “by non-enmity” in the Pāli and Patna, “by not resenting” in the Chinese, but “by patience” (kṣānti) in the Sanskrit and Tibetan.

    A negation (simply not returning enmity) against a named virtue to cultivate (patience). For practice, one says what to stop; the other what to develop.

  • N-003 7.5, 8.5: What overthrows the undisciplined person is Māra in the Pāli and Patna, but passion (rāga) in the Gāndhārī, Sanskrit and Tibetan.

    A personified tempter against a plain mental state. The same verse can read as mythic or psychological depending on the tradition.

  • N-004 7.4, 8.4: Where the Pāli praises faith and energy, the Sanskrit speaks of wakefulness and the Tibetan of mindfulness; the Chinese and Tibetan drop faith.

    The list of qualities that protect a practitioner shifts from faith toward attentiveness in the northern texts.

  • N-005 9.3, 10.3: The worthy wearer of the robe has self-control and truth (Pāli, Patna) or self-control and gentleness (Gāndhārī, Sanskrit, Tibetan).

    Two different virtues define the true renunciant: truthfulness, or gentleness of manner.

  • N-006 3.1, 4.1: The remembered injury includes a blow (“he struck me”) in the Pāli and Patna; in the Sanskrit, Tibetan and the related Chinese it is all words (“they spoke against me”).

    The teaching on letting go of grudges applies either way; the Pāli makes the injury physical, the northern texts verbal.

  • N-007 13.3, 14.3: Only the Gāndhārī says the mind is “unguarded / well guarded”; every other tradition says “undeveloped / well developed”.

    Guarding (a protective practice) against developing (a cultivating one). A lone reading, so it does not change the Blend, but it shows a different emphasis.

  • N-008 1.6: The Tibetan ends verse 1 with “like a head cut off by a wheel”; its own Sanskrit source and every other tradition have the wheel following the ox's foot.

    A translation event inside the Tibetan, not an older reading. A reminder that a translation can carry its own errors or reinterpretations.

  • N-009 19.1, 20.1, 17.3, 18.3, 19.3: The Chinese recasts several lines in moral terms: “clever words, many wants”, “that is self-made calamity / self-made blessing”, a herd of cattle instead of a cowherd counting others' cows.

    The 3rd-century Chinese translator reads the verses as plain ethical advice in his own idiom. It shows how one early reader understood them.

  • N-010 20.3, 20.4, 20.5: The Pāli's portrait of the practitioner who lives the teaching (letting go of passion, hatred and delusion; fully aware; mind released; clinging to nothing) has no counterpart in the Gāndhārī or Udānavarga, whose versions are shorter.

    The fuller description of liberation may be an expansion in the Pāli (Patna shares only part of it), or a loss elsewhere. The Blend keeps it (R-T3-04).
