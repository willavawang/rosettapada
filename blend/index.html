Rulings and rules waiting for you

R-T2-01T2

How to say dhammā in verses 1–2

In verses 1–2 the plural dhammā means what the mind goes before: our states of mind and what we experience. The Chinese 法 'all things' and the Tibetan 'phenomena' translate the same word, so they agree; the Chinese 道 'the Way' elsewhere is not a separate reading. Both readers advise against “experience”, the current placeholder's word, as adding an interpretation.

Verses: 1, 2

R-T2-02T2

“This is an age-old principle”, not “eternal law” (verse 5)

Verse 5 ends esa dhammo sanantano. Sanantano means 'from of old, perpetual'. “Eternal” asserts eternity, a metaphysical claim; “age-old” says only that it is ancient. Neither the Tibetan ('the nature of things') nor the Chinese ('this way is worth honouring') says eternal. Both readers recommend “age-old”.

Verses: 5

R-T2-03T2

vera: “enmity”, not “hatred” (verses 3–5)

Vera is enmity between people, a grudge held against someone; the Chinese 怨 'resentment' and the Tibetan 'grudge' agree. “Hatred” is better kept for dosa, the inner poison, which appears elsewhere. The placeholder says “hostility”.

Verses: 3, 4, 5

R-T2-04T2

Keep the pun on the stained robe (verses 9–10)

The Pāli plays on kasāva 'stain, impurity' and kāsāva 'the ochre robe' (literally the dyed one): someone still stained wears the stained robe. The Chinese and Tibetan lose the pun. Literal+ allows a departure where the original has a beauty a plain rendering loses.

Verses: 9, 10

R-T2-06T2

Same idea, different word: follow the widest support

Often the traditions say the same thing with a different word (Pāli 'never leaving' vs 'following' for the shadow in verse 2). Default: use the word with the widest support across independent traditions; on a tie, keep the Pāli word. In Pairs this changes only verse 2: “like a shadow that follows”.

Verses: 2, 7, 8, 11, 13, 14, 16

R-T2-07T2

dhamma as the Buddha's teaching (verse 20)

Where dhamma means the teaching itself, say so in plain English, or keep the word Dhamma untranslated.

Verses: 20

R-T3-01T3

Count traditions by family, not by column

The Tibetan translates the Sanskrit Udānavarga, so the two count as one voice. Where the Tibetan alone departs from its Sanskrit, it is a Tibetan translation matter and is not followed. Example: in verse 1 the Tibetan has “like a head cut off by a wheel” where everyone else has the wheel following the ox.

Verses: 1, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 13, 14, 15

R-T3-02T3

A lone voice gives way

A reading found in only one tradition gives way to a different reading shared by two or more others. It is recorded, not followed. A fragment of a damaged manuscript can confirm a reading but never carry one alone. Example: the Gāndhārī alone says “unguarded” where all others say “undeveloped” (verses 13–14).

Verses: 1, 8, 13, 14, 16, 20

R-T3-03T3

The Chinese counts where it agrees, informs where it recasts

The Chinese translator wrote in his own idiom. Where his words carry the same sense, that counts as agreement. Where he changes an image or a line (grass for a weak tree in verse 7; a herd of cattle in verse 19), it shows how an early reader understood the verse but does not replace what the Indic texts agree on.

Verses: 3, 7, 8, 9, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20

R-T3-04T3

Partial parallels and missing lines inform, not supply

A partial parallel (a different verse that shares some lines) can confirm a reading but never adds or removes a line. The Blend keeps every Pāli line; where other traditions lack a line, that is noted, not treated as evidence against it.

Verses: 4, 6, 17, 18, 20

R-T3-05T3

When Pāli and Patna stand against Gāndhārī, Sanskrit and Tibetan

Pāli and Patna are close relatives, and so are the Gāndhārī and the Udānavarga (Sanskrit and Tibetan). When the two groups disagree on a word that changes the meaning, the evidence is roughly even. Pairs has four such places (verses 3–4, 7–8, 9–10 and 19–20), listed below as T4 questions. Both readers advise deciding these one by one for now and revisiting once we see how often the pattern recurs.

T4-01T4

“Made of mind” or “swift as mind”? (verses 1–2, line 2)

Pāli manomayā 'made of mind' stands alone. Patna, Gāndhārī, Sanskrit and Tibetan all read manojavā 'swift as mind'; the Chinese 心使 is unclear. By rule R-T3-02 the lone Pāli would give way, but this is one of the most quoted lines in the book, so it comes to you.

Claude: The evidence favours B. A is the reading most readers will know.

Lines: 1.2, 2.2

T4-02T4

“Struck me” or “spoke against me”? (verses 3–4, line 1)

Pāli and Patna: 'he abused me, he struck me'. The Sanskrit, Tibetan and the Chinese of the same family: 'they abused me, they spoke against me', all verbal. The Gāndhārī lacks the verse.

Claude: A: two traditions against one family voice, and the physical blow sharpens the list.

Lines: 3.1, 4.1

T4-03T4

What does yamāmase mean? (verse 6, line 2)

A famous crux: 'Others do not understand that here we ___.' The Pāli commentary reads 'we are perishing'; the word can also mean 'we must restrain ourselves'. Patna's jayāmatha is unclear, and the partial Chinese and Tibetan parallels speak of striving.

Claude: Open. A fits the verse's logic (seeing our mortality ends quarrels); B and C fit the parallels.

Lines: 6.2

T4-04T4

Māra or passion? (verses 7–8, line 5)

Pāli and Patna: Māra overpowers him, as wind a weak tree. Gāndhārī, Sanskrit and Tibetan: passion overpowers him. The Chinese 邪 'the wicked' could support either.

Claude: Open: two families, one each.

Lines: 7.5, 8.5

T4-05T4

“Self-control and truth” or “self-control and gentleness”? (verses 9–10, line 3)

Pāli and Patna: damasacca, self-control and truth. Gāndhārī, Sanskrit and Tibetan: self-control and gentleness.

Claude: Open.

Lines: 9.3, 10.3

T4-06T4

What is recited? (verses 19–20, line 1)

'Though he recites much saṁhita…' The word can mean the collected scriptures, or meaningful, well-connected words (the Tibetan reads 'reasoned speech'), or what is beneficial. The verse contrasts reciting with practising.

Claude: A: the contrast between reciting texts and living them is the point of the pair.

Lines: 19.1, 20.1

T4-07T4

A share in “the recluse's life” or in “its goal”? (verses 19–20, last line)

Pāli and Patna: he has no share in the recluse's life (sāmañña). Gāndhārī and Sanskrit: no share in the goal of the recluse's life.

Claude: A, slightly: the shorter line, and B's 'goal' may be an explanatory expansion.

Lines: 19.4, 20.6

What we are learning

The record behind the home page’s account of how the traditions differ. Patterns stay hypotheses until later chapters support or weaken them.

Patterns

  • P-01 hypothesis Where the Pāli has a negation or a general quality, the Udānavarga (Sanskrit and Tibetan) tends to name a specific virtue or practice to cultivate: patience, wakefulness, mindfulness. Evidence: N-002, N-004 · chapters 1
  • P-02 hypothesis Pāli and Patna keep mythic or concrete images (Māra; a blow) where the Gāndhārī and Udānavarga have psychological or verbal ones (passion; words). Evidence: N-003, N-006 · chapters 1
  • P-03 hypothesis The Pāli can be the outlier, even in its best-known lines; agreement among the others is a real check on it. Evidence: N-001 · chapters 1
  • P-04 hypothesis The Chinese is better read as an early interpretation than as a separate wording of the verse: it moralizes and uses its own images. Evidence: N-009 · chapters 1
  • P-05 hypothesis Many Tibetan departures are translation events (reordering, formulaic lines, misreadings), not older readings. Evidence: N-008 · chapters 1

Differences that matter

  • N-001 doctrine 1.2, 2.2

    The Pāli alone says the mind's states are “made of mind” (manomayā). Patna, Gāndhārī, Sanskrit and Tibetan all say “swift as mind” (manojavā).

    The most quoted line of the Dhammapada is, on this evidence, the outlier. “Made of mind” invites the reading that mind constructs experience; “swift as mind” stresses the speed with which mind leads. The teaching that mind comes first stands in every tradition either way.

    T4-01

  • N-002 practice 5.3

    Enmity is ended “by non-enmity” in the Pāli and Patna, “by not resenting” in the Chinese, but “by patience” (kṣānti) in the Sanskrit and Tibetan.

    A negation (simply not returning enmity) against a named virtue to cultivate (patience). For practice, one says what to stop; the other what to develop.

    R-T3-01

  • N-003 doctrine 7.5, 8.5

    What overthrows the undisciplined person is Māra in the Pāli and Patna, but passion (rāga) in the Gāndhārī, Sanskrit and Tibetan.

    A personified tempter against a plain mental state. The same verse can read as mythic or psychological depending on the tradition.

    T4-04

  • N-004 practice 7.4, 8.4

    Where the Pāli praises faith and energy, the Sanskrit speaks of wakefulness and the Tibetan of mindfulness; the Chinese and Tibetan drop faith.

    The list of qualities that protect a practitioner shifts from faith toward attentiveness in the northern texts.

  • N-005 practice 9.3, 10.3

    The worthy wearer of the robe has self-control and truth (Pāli, Patna) or self-control and gentleness (Gāndhārī, Sanskrit, Tibetan).

    Two different virtues define the true renunciant: truthfulness, or gentleness of manner.

    T4-05

  • N-006 imagery 3.1, 4.1

    The remembered injury includes a blow (“he struck me”) in the Pāli and Patna; in the Sanskrit, Tibetan and the related Chinese it is all words (“they spoke against me”).

    The teaching on letting go of grudges applies either way; the Pāli makes the injury physical, the northern texts verbal.

    T4-02

  • N-007 practice 13.3, 14.3

    Only the Gāndhārī says the mind is “unguarded / well guarded”; every other tradition says “undeveloped / well developed”.

    Guarding (a protective practice) against developing (a cultivating one). A lone reading, so it does not change the Blend, but it shows a different emphasis.

    R-T3-02

  • N-008 how it was translated 1.6

    The Tibetan ends verse 1 with “like a head cut off by a wheel”; its own Sanskrit source and every other tradition have the wheel following the ox's foot.

    A translation event inside the Tibetan, not an older reading. A reminder that a translation can carry its own errors or reinterpretations.

    R-T3-01

  • N-009 how it was translated 19.1, 20.1, 17.3, 18.3, 19.3

    The Chinese recasts several lines in moral terms: “clever words, many wants”, “that is self-made calamity / self-made blessing”, a herd of cattle instead of a cowherd counting others' cows.

    The 3rd-century Chinese translator reads the verses as plain ethical advice in his own idiom. It shows how one early reader understood them.

    R-T3-03

  • N-010 doctrine 20.3, 20.4, 20.5

    The Pāli's portrait of the practitioner who lives the teaching (letting go of passion, hatred and delusion; fully aware; mind released; clinging to nothing) has no counterpart in the Gāndhārī or Udānavarga, whose versions are shorter.

    The fuller description of liberation may be an expansion in the Pāli (Patna shares only part of it), or a loss elsewhere. The Blend keeps it (R-T3-04).
