Reading notes

What you are reading

The reader follows all 26 chapters of the Pāli Dhammapada, covering 423 verse positions. Alongside the Pāli are selected Patna, Gāndhārī, Sanskrit, Chinese, and Tibetan parallels. The collections have their own order and wording.

The default Rosettapada English is an editorial translation based on the Mahāsaṅgīti Pāli. The rendering draws on the historical parallels and Bhikkhu Sujato’s Sayings of the Dhamma as a reference. Where the witnesses diverge, the English generally follows Pāli and the notes record significant alternatives. The Pāli and Sujato reference edition are retained from the pinned CC0 SuttaCentral snapshot. The earlier two-verse sample remains available as an archival draft.

English reading notes

Choose the English source from the menu beneath the English column heading. Alternate editorial translations follow the displayed Patna, Gāndhārī, Sanskrit, Classical Chinese, or Tibetan readings where available. These translations cover every displayed reading in all twenty-six chapters. Each line translates the corresponding line of that witness, read with its whole verse, and keeps the witness’s own wording where it differs from the Pāli. In the translations, […] marks words lost in a damaged manuscript. Words in square brackets are restored where the surviving letters or a close parallel make them all but certain, and words in angle brackets ⟨ ⟩ are supplied where the text appears to have dropped them. A dash in place of a whole verse means the tradition has no parallel at that position. When a chapter has no translation from the preferred source, the reader shows the Pāli-based English and retains the preference.

The reading notes explain selected translation choices and recensional differences. The endnotes by Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu provide further context for several difficult Pāli readings.

  • Pāli 1 · The Pāli describes experience as mind-made. Patna, Gāndhārī, and Sanskrit use a form corresponding to mind-impelled. The Tibetan counterpart ends with a wheel cutting off a head. The English keeps the Pāli ox and wheel.
  • Pāli 5 · Avera is the absence of hostility. The Sanskrit kṣānti and Tibetan bzod pa give patience or forbearance as the means by which hostility subsides.
  • Pāli 6 · Yamāmase has been read in relation to dying and to restraint. This draft follows the mortality reading. Sujato understands it as restraining ourselves. The provisional Tibetan selection concerns settling conflict through gentle speech.
  • Pāli 8 · Asubha in this meditation context concerns attending to the unattractive aspects of the body to loosen sensual attachment. See Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu’s end note on 7–8.
  • Pāli 21 · The deathless (amata) names nibbāna in this context. The contrast is between heedfulness on the path of release and the living heedlessness of one compared to the dead.
  • Pāli 25 · Dīpa may mean island or lamp. The flood supports island here. Chinese T210 uses illumination imagery.
  • Pāli 30 · Maghavā is a name of Sakka, also known as Indra. The selected Chinese parallel at this position gives a different illustration of heedfulness.
  • Pāli 39 · Puñña and pāpa refer to merit and evil. The traditional reading concerns an arahant whose actions are free of the conditions for further karmic results. The Chinese passage develops a different contrast.
  • Pāli 40 · The Pāli begins with the body as a clay pot. The selected Chinese counterpart opens with the image of a tortoise guarding its faculties.
  • Pāli 44 · Vicessati admits different readings. Here it is rendered as discerning or examining. The flower image describes choosing a well-taught saying.
  • Pāli 45 · Pāli sekho, Patna śekho, and Sanskrit śaikṣaḥ all name the trainee. The Gāndhārī counterpart instead has budhu, the awakened one.
  • Pāli 46 · The foam simile applies to the body in Pāli, Gāndhārī, and Sanskrit. Patna applies it to this world.
  • Pāli 70 · Eating from a grass tip evokes severe ascetic restraint. The verse weighs that outward austerity against understanding the teaching.
  • Pāli 71 · The Pāli muccati is difficult in this image. This draft follows the curdling interpretation supported by the comparative readings. Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu instead understands the Pāli as the deed’s result not coming out at once.
  • Pāli 79 · The Pāli can be understood as drinking in the Dhamma or taking joy in it. The Patna text supports the drinking image. Gāndhārī and Sanskrit support the sense of joy.
  • Pāli 97 · The stanza plays with expressions that can sound like accusations. Knowing the unmade is a positive reading of akataññū. Freedom from dependence on faith and the cutting of the link point to direct knowledge and the end of renewed becoming.
  • Pāli 100 · The Tibetan parallel has a hundred stanzas instead of a thousand words. Both value the effect of hearing over the quantity recited.
  • Pāli 105 · Gandhabba refers to a class of celestial beings associated with music. Verses 104–105 form a single argument.
  • Pāli 106 · Pāli praises honoring a cultivated person. The Chinese counterpart praises recollection of the Dhamma, while a related Tibetan passage praises faith in the Buddha.
  • Pāli 107 · The Chinese counterpart names the Three Honored Ones in the place where Pāli speaks of honoring a cultivated person.
  • Pāli 110 · The Tibetan parallel speaks of a few days, as do several of its following counterparts in this sequence.
  • Pāli 114 · The Deathless (amata) is an epithet for nibbāna. The Tibetan counterpart refers to the unchanging state.
  • Pāli 115 · The recorded Tibetan counterpart speaks of seeing a state difficult to see and contrasts a century with a few days.
  • Pāli 124 · The last line concerns what accrues from one’s own conduct. The preceding pair, 119–120, explicitly allows that a person doing good may still encounter adversity.
  • Pāli 126 · “Fully extinguished” renders parinibbanti. Its subject is the extinguishing of the fires of attachment, aversion, and delusion.
  • Pāli 129 · The Patna and Sanskrit counterparts give the second line as the love of life, like Pāli verse 130.
  • Pāli 134 · The Pāli gong can be understood as broken and therefore silent. The Sanskrit comparison and the second Patna stanza use the image of an unstruck gong. Patna also gives the opposite case in a separate stanza.
  • Pāli 142 · Several parallels say “lives by the Dhamma” where Pāli has samaṁ, living equably or uprightly. The contrast is between outward dress and conduct.
  • Pāli 143 · The English follows the reproach reading nindaṁ in Ānandajoti’s comparative edition. The pinned Pāli reads niddaṁ, allowing a reading about rousing from sleep.
  • Pāli 145 · The Sanskrit counterpart closes with the wise taming themselves. Pāli here names those of good observance, where verse 80 names the wise.
  • Pāli 154 · The traditional reading understands the house-builder as craving. The dismantled house carries that meaning through the image. The Sanskrit counterpart retains the building image and ends without explicitly naming craving.
  • Pāli 157 · The night’s three watches can be read literally. Traditional interpretation also takes them as stages of life.
  • Pāli 159 · The Derge Tibetan counterpart ends in the joy of taming oneself. The Pāli closes on how difficult the work is.
  • Pāli 160 · Here attā works as the ordinary reflexive “oneself.” The Chinese counterpart frames the matter as training oneself to be of benefit to others.
  • Pāli 164 · The bamboo image is Pāli and Tibetan. The Chinese counterpart uses the image of bitter seed.
  • Pāli 166 · The traditional context takes one’s true good to be progress on the path. The Chinese counterpart emphasizes benefiting oneself and others together.
  • Pāli 167 · “Add to the round of the world” reads lokavaḍḍhano in the context of perpetuating saṁsāra. The expression is more literally “one who increases the world.”
  • Pāli 173 · “Covered over” preserves the verb pidhīyati and the image of light breaking through. The verse affirms a change of conduct.
  • Pāli 178 · The stream is entry into the noble path. Patna names gaining human birth among the comparisons, where Pāli names dominion over all worlds.
  • Pāli 183 · Some parallels give the last line in the singular, as the teaching of the Buddha. Pāli uses the plural.
  • Pāli 185 · The monastic code is the Pātimokkha. The list joins bodily and verbal conduct with cultivation of the mind.
  • Pāli 189 · Patna 217 lacks the negative in its third line, in Cone’s edition as in the comparative edition. The negation of its first two lines evidently carries over, and the Patna English supplies it in angle brackets.
  • Pāli 200 · Patna and Gāndhārī keep the contrast between those who have something and those who have nothing. Pāli and Sanskrit also give the radiant gods and their food of joy.
  • Pāli 202 · The aggregates (khandhā) are form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness. The line situates suffering in the constituents of lived experience.
  • Pāli 209 · Patna and Sanskrit give the final object as those devoted to the aim. The pinned Pāli has attānuyoginaṁ, those devoted to training themselves.
  • Pāli 211 · Here Pāli names parting from what is dear. Sanskrit names the state of holding something dear. Both use the image of bonds.
  • Pāli 216 · Verses 212–216 retain their distinct opening terms: what is dear, affectionate attachment, relishing, sensual desire, and craving.
  • Pāli 218 · “The unspoken” traditionally points to nibbāna. “Upstream” also names a kind of non-returner in the early texts.
  • Pāli 221 · “Name and form” renders nāmarūpa, a technical pairing for the mental and bodily aspects of experience. “Fetters” keeps the image of bonds that tie beings to suffering.
  • Pāli 226 · Nibbāna is the goal of liberation. “Taints” renders āsavā, the deep tendencies whose exhaustion marks liberation.
  • Pāli 230 · The Pāli, Patna 287, and Gāndhārī 242 praise the person with an image of Jambu River gold and name the gods and Brahmā. The Tibetan excerpt at Derge 72, 240a.6 keeps the gold and blame lines. Chinese T210 25.11 invokes an arhat’s purity and praise by Brahmā and Śakra.
  • Pāli 236 · Dīpa can mean “lamp” or “island” in Pāli. The Sanskrit parallel to the repeated line in verse 238 (Udānavarga 16.3) has dvīpa, “island.” The island is a place of refuge.
  • Pāli 238 · The Sanskrit Udānavarga 16.3 joins the island and freedom-from-rebirth lines to a smith’s removal of dross, an image Pāli places in the next verse.
  • Pāli 240 · Atidhonacārin is difficult. The English follows the sense of transgressing purity, discussed in Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu’s note on verse 240. Sanskrit Udānavarga 9.19 describes a person who acts without reflection.
  • Pāli 243 · The Pāli singles out ignorance as the supreme stain. Patna 159 explicitly names ignorance and death.
  • Pāli 251 · Sanskrit Udānavarga 29.37 has a flood of sensual desire where the Pāli has the fire of passion. Both keep the net of delusion and the river of craving.
  • Pāli 254 · “Outside the path” follows the practice context explained in DN 16: the kinds of contemplative realization are found where the noble eightfold path is practiced. Papañca is the proliferation or elaboration of thought.
  • Pāli 255 · Sanskrit Udānavarga 29.38 shares the opening lines with Pāli 254–255 and follows them with the proliferation lines found in Pāli 254.
  • Pāli 259 · The Pāli says passati, “sees,” with the body. Patna 32, Gāndhārī 114, and Sanskrit Udānavarga 4.21 use verbs for touching or contacting the teaching. The bodily expression points to direct experience.
  • Pāli 265 · The Pāli links sameti, “stills,” and samaṇa, “contemplative,” by sound. Sanskrit Udānavarga 33.8 uses a related pattern to explain who merits the name brahmin.
  • Pāli 266 · The reading “ways of household life” is supported by Gāndhārī 67 and Sanskrit Udānavarga 32.18. The Mahāvastu parallel has a different expression, viṣamāṁ dharmāṁ, for an uneven or improper way of conduct.
  • Pāli 272 · This reads verses 271–272 as one warning against complacency, following Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu’s explanation of the construction. Sanskrit Udānavarga 32.32 changes “joy of renunciation” to “joy of awakening.”
  • Pāli 275 · The Pāli aññāya can be read as “having understood” or “for understanding,” as Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu notes at verse 275. The Sanskrit Udānavarga 12.9 places the arrow image beside the instruction to do the work oneself.
  • Pāli 278 · The refrain describes conditioned things as subject to dukkha. The following verse uses a wider term, dhammā, for phenomena.
  • Pāli 282 · The Pāli speaks of wisdom growing through practice. The Sanskrit parallel, Udānavarga 29.40, speaks instead of becoming arising through bonds and ending through release from them.
  • Pāli 283 · The forest evokes entanglement in desire, as the calf simile in the next verse illustrates. Pāli nibbanā, “without a forest,” also echoes nibbāna.
  • Pāli 284 · Pāli names a man’s attachment to women. Patna 362 and Sanskrit Udānavarga 18.4 use kin or relations, broadening the object of attachment.
  • Pāli 290 · The comparison in Pāli, Patna 77, and Sanskrit Udānavarga 30.30 turns on a limited happiness set against a greater one. The English preserves that scale.
  • Pāli 294 · The Dhammapada commentary reads the shocking images figuratively: mother as craving, father as conceit, and the two kings as eternalism and annihilationism. See Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu’s note on verse 294. Chinese T210 2.12 has a different cast of mother, ruler, ministers, and retinue.
  • Pāli 295 · The Dhammapada commentary reads the tiger image in connection with doubt or the five hindrances. See Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu’s note on verse 295. Sanskrit Udānavarga 33.62 closes by calling the person pure. The Tibetan at Derge 72, 252a.7–252b.1 calls such a person a brahmin without wrongdoing.
  • Pāli 296 · Pāli 296–298 present three separate recollections. Chinese T210 6.11 gathers Buddha, Dharma, and Saṅgha into one stanza, shown beside all three Pāli positions.
  • Pāli 297 · The Chinese text shared by verses 296–298 is T210 6.11. The Pāli, Gāndhārī 101, Sanskrit Udānavarga 15.13, and Tibetan at Derge 72, 222b.1–222b.2 have a separate recollection of the Dhamma.
  • Pāli 298 · The shared Chinese T210 6.11 stanza recollects the three together. This English follows the separate Pāli recollection of the Saṅgha.
  • Pāli 302 · The traveler image is read here in the context of suffering and continued wandering. Sanskrit Udānavarga 11.8 and Tibetan Derge 72, 219a.4 end with the suffering of repeated becoming rather than the Pāli’s concluding exhortation.
  • Pāli 305 · Pāli damayaṁ describes taming oneself. Patna 313 and Sanskrit Udānavarga 23.2 have forms of ram, suggesting enjoyment or delight in solitude.
  • Pāli 310 · Where the Pāli closes with advice to refrain, Patna 211 and Sanskrit Udānavarga 4.15b close with a rebirth in hell.
  • Pāli 311 · Pāli and Patna 296 name kusa grass. Gāndhārī 215 and Sanskrit Udānavarga 11.4 use a different grass term in the same cutting-hand simile.
  • Pāli 316 · Patna 169, Gāndhārī 273, and Sanskrit Udānavarga 16.4 bring together the shame and danger contrasts that Pāli gives as two verses, 316 and 317.
  • Pāli 323 · The Pāli repeats danta, “tamed,” and says attanā sudantena, “by a well-tamed self.” The destination is the place still unvisited.
  • Pāli 327 · Sanskrit Udānavarga 4.27 and 4.36 distribute the heedfulness, guarding the mind, and elephant-in-a-bog images across two verses.
  • Pāli 334 · Māluvā is a climbing vine. The Pāli and Sanskrit Udānavarga 3.4 follow the heedless person through the monkey image. The feminine sā in Patna 137 makes craving itself the likelier subject of the leaping line.
  • Pāli 337 · Vetiver is a fragrant grass whose roots are dug up for use. Patna 140 and Sanskrit Udānavarga 3.11 end by asking what sorrow or fear can remain when craving is uprooted. The reed and Māra belong to the Pāli ending. Gāndhārī 126 shares the address to those gathered, then urges heedfulness.
  • Pāli 339 · The stanza leaves the thirty-six streams unexplained. Pāli commentarial traditions count three kinds of craving across the six inner and six outer sense fields, with different accounts of which three kinds are intended. See Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu’s endnote on verse 339.
  • Pāli 344 · The Pāli plays on vana, forest and the tangle of desire. The wording can evoke a renunciant who goes to the forest and then rushes back into attachment. The last line makes that return explicit.
  • Pāli 346 · Pāli sithila means slack or loose, which gives the bond its paradox. Patna 144 calls it subtle and ends with all suffering abandoned. Sanskrit Udānavarga 2.6 describes the bond as firm on all sides.
  • Pāli 347 · The Gāndhārī counterpart 171 shares the spider image and closes with sensual pleasure abandoned. The Pāli closes with all suffering abandoned.
  • Pāli 348 · The Pāli says in front, behind, and in the middle. The Dhammapada commentary reads these as the aggregates of the past, future, and present. The English follows that temporal reading while preserving becoming and rebirth in the final lines.
  • Pāli 350 · Attention to the unattractive renders asubha, a contemplation of the body’s less alluring aspects used to loosen sensual attachment. See Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu’s notes to verses 7–8 and 350.
  • Pāli 353 · In MN 26 the Buddha speaks these lines in response to Upaka asking whose teaching he follows. Sanskrit Udānavarga 21.1 says he is freed from all fear where Pāli speaks of freedom through craving’s ending.
  • Pāli 355 · The Pāli contrasts the foolish with those who seek the farther shore. Sanskrit Udānavarga 2.16 uses ātmagaveṣin, one who searches for oneself, in that line.
  • Pāli 365 · Salābha means one’s own gains here. Sanskrit Udānavarga 13.8 makes this explicit with svalābha. Patna 55 and Gāndhārī 61 support the same reading. The surrounding verses put the gains in the context of monastic support.
  • Pāli 366 · Patna 56 and Gāndhārī 62 preserve the contrast between receiving little and valuing one’s own gains. Chinese T210 34.6 stresses receiving little without accumulating it.
  • Pāli 367 · Name and form translates nāmarūpa, a term for the mental and physical aspects of experience. The opening of Sanskrit Udānavarga 32.17 instead emphasizes an absence of accumulation.
  • Pāli 369 · The verse leaves the boat image open. The Dhammapada commentary identifies the boat with one’s own person and the water with unskillful thoughts. See Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu’s endnote on verse 369.
  • Pāli 370 · The verse itself leaves the groups of five unnamed. The Dhammapada commentary identifies five lower fetters to cut, five higher fetters to abandon, five spiritual faculties to develop, and five attachments to transcend. See Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu’s endnote on verse 370.
  • Pāli 372 · Absorption renders jhāna, meditative collectedness. Concentration in verse 365 renders samādhi. Here the verse sets jhāna and wisdom alongside one another.
  • Pāli 374 · Aggregates refers to form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness. Sanskrit Udānavarga 32.10 likewise speaks of seeing their arising and passing, but ends with a glad and mindful mendicant going forth. The Pāli ends with the deathless.
  • Pāli 380 · The repetition of attā is reflexive here, urging a person to train and protect themselves. Patna 322 also has a merchant and fine horse. Sanskrit Udānavarga 19.14 gives the horse to a charioteer.
  • Pāli 383 · The chapter repeatedly uses brahmin, an inherited social designation, to name the awakened or exemplary person. Here it can be an address to a brahmin in the ordinary sense. Verses 393 and 396 make the chapter’s distinction between birth and conduct explicit.
  • Pāli 384 · The Pāli phrase literally names two qualities without specifying them. The Dhammapada commentary takes them as calm and insight. Sanskrit Udānavarga 33.72 refers instead to one’s own qualities or practices.
  • Pāli 388 · The stanza uses sound to connect brahmin with banishing evil, ascetic with even conduct, and going forth with sending away one’s stains. These are wordplays within the verse.
  • Pāli 393 · The Pāli and Sanskrit Udānavarga 33.7 end with truth and Dhamma. Patna 37 and Gāndhārī 1 end with banishing wrongdoing, small and great.
  • Pāli 396 · Bho is a conventional form of address associated here with brahmin status. Calling someone a bho-speaker leaves them with a social marker while the refrain reserves its sense of brahmin for one without clinging.
  • Pāli 398 · The verse gives a series of bonds and a crossbar. The Dhammapada commentary reads the equipment as figures for resentment, craving, views, and obsessions. See Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu’s endnote on verse 398.
  • Pāli 405 · The rod is a figure for violence. The ancient pair tasesu and thāvaresu can evoke the trembling and steadfast or the moving and stationary. The line reaches across those categories.
  • Pāli 410 · Expectations renders āsā, anticipatory longing. The stanza describes release from dependence on what this world or a next one might bring.
  • Pāli 412 · Merit and wrongdoing are paired as bonds that the liberated person has gone beyond. Sanskrit Udānavarga 33.29 shares that pairing and closes by emphasizing freedom from attachment.
  • Pāli 420 · Gandhabbas are celestial beings. The Pāli’s arahant is one whose taints are exhausted. Sanskrit Udānavarga 33.46 describes the person as endowed with boundless knowledge.
  • Pāli 422 · The bull is an ancient image of preeminence. The Sanskrit Udānavarga 33.50 keeps the bull image and uses nāga where the Pāli has a hero.
  • Pāli 423 · The verse gathers knowledge of former lives, the destinations of beings, and the ending of birth. Sanskrit Udānavarga 33.47 names knowing suffering’s end in its penultimate line.

Chapter titles and line breaks

Yamakavagga is the chapter of pairs or twin verses; Appamādavagga concerns heedfulness, also rendered diligence or earnestness; Cittavagga concerns the mind, called thought in some older translations. These English titles translate the traditional Pāli chapter names. Our second page uses “Heedfulness”; Sujato’s source heading is “Diligence.”

Each verse begins at the same horizontal level across the sheet. Each tradition retains its own line divisions rather than being forced into the English number of lines. The Pāli follows the selected SuttaCentral edition. The Pāli-based English uses its own reading lines; the alternate translations follow their source line for line. The historical parallels follow the sources below. Line breaks are editorial presentation, not a reconstruction of an original manuscript. Commentary-derived story headings, chapter colophons, and nonprinting formatting markers are omitted.

Alignment is at verse level. Word order, length, imagery, and even the scope of a parallel can differ. The correspondence is not word by word.

Gaps and uncertainties

A dash means no parallel was located in the consulted sources. It does not mean that a tradition has no related teaching. Some Gāndhārī and Patna passages are physically fragmentary; their surviving words and lacunae are retained. Other complete passages correspond only in part. The source table gives available details on those cases.

Dotted underlining marks uncertain or conjecturally restored words in the consulted Indic edition; hovering shows which. Its comparison emphasis is not treated as a conjecture. Legacy private-use transliteration glyphs are displayed as their standard Unicode equivalents, verified against the source font.

Chinese and pinyin

The Chinese column uses the Faju jing (T210) where a counterpart is identified. Where a counterpart is not located in T210, it uses a documented parallel from the Chuyao jing (T212) or Faji yaosong jing (T213); each exact reference is listed below. These recensions are not silently treated as the same collection.

Chinese pinyin is a modern Mandarin reading aid for every displayed Chinese passage, hidden by default. It is not a historical witness or a reconstruction of ancient pronunciation. It can be shown independently of the characters. Rare and context-sensitive readings remain editorial choices.

Tibetan contents

Among the six pilot chapters’ 88 Pāli positions, 82 have directly checked Tibetan counterparts, 4 have partial or provisional related selections, and 2 remain unlocated. The full reader displays 339 Tibetan selections. Each has a Derge source span. Present means a sourced historical counterpart and can include differences in wording, imagery, and scope.

The independent Tibetan–Pāli concordance and the native checks establish at least 333 of the full 423 Pāli verse positions with Tibetan parallels. This is a lower bound. The comparative sample

Lines in the earlier multilingual sample form a provisional correspondence by sense, not a verified line-by-line concordance. Its spelling, punctuation, source numbering, and transcriptions still require editorial verification. Its English is a working rendering of Pāli and does not translate all six traditions.

The sample labels its witnesses as Pāli Dhp 1–2; Patna PDhp 1–2; Gāndhārī GDhp 201–202; Sanskrit Udānavarga 31.23–24; and Chinese Faju jing, T210 9.1–2. These are inherited references, retained for review.

A difference worth keeping visible

Pāli verse 1 has manomayā (“made by mind”), alongside forms of manojavā in the other Indian witnesses. The Tibetan describes mind as swift. Its final image is a wheel cutting off a head, while the Pāli compares suffering following action to the wheel following an ox’s foot. Those are differences in the consulted texts, not harmonizations supplied for this site. Switch the English source beneath the English heading to compare the alternate drafts.
